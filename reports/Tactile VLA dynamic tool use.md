# Close the Kilohertz Loop on Tool Impacts

No published system, product or repository as of September 2026 combines a slow vision-language-action (VLA) or world-model policy with a high-frequency tactile- and torque-driven controller that regulates grip force, grip position and applied torque during hammer strikes or power-drill engagement; a six-criterion screen of roughly sixty candidates finds a ceiling of about four criteria met by any one work, and the field's own curated index of force/tactile VLAs contains "no papers … mention tool use, hammers, nails, drills, screws" ([Awesome-Force-Tactile-VLA](https://github.com/OpenHelix-Team/Awesome-Force-Tactile-VLA)). Robots do hammer nails and drive screws today — Tool-as-Interface hits 13/13 from RGB alone, Grasp-to-Act drives a nail with 100% success over four real strikes, and Physical Intelligence's RL Token drives M3 screws with an electric screwdriver — but every one treats the tool as a rigid extension of the gripper or corrects slip from proprioception at 30 Hz; none senses the strike or the bit engagement through the hand and none closes a force loop on it ([Tool-as-Interface](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04612); [Grasp-to-Act](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20466); [RL Token](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23073)). The user's idea is therefore meaningfully different, but not where it was framed: the "two degrees of separation" (robot to hammer to nail) is shared by every neighbor, whereas closing a kilohertz-class loop on tool–target interaction sensed *through* the tool — with joint effort as a first-class input, a grip-force/stiffness action space, and a learned engagement-state estimate — has no precedent. The physics fixes the design: a hammer–nail pulse lasts about 3 ms and impulsive loads rise in under 20 ms, so only prediction can protect the grasp (humans pre-scale grip ~150 ms before a self-produced impact and react to slip only after 74 ± 9 ms), while VLA observation-to-action latency is 100–250 ms and the fastest learned tactile loops run at 24–100 Hz ([Delevoye-Turrell et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/02724980245000025); [Johansson & Westling 1988](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00247523); [Johansson & Westling 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3582528); [Real-Time Chunking](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07339)). The viable foundational method is a three-layer stack: a 1–10 Hz VLA that emits task phase, strike target and predicted-load parameters; a 100–200 Hz learned reactive layer consuming kilohertz wrist force/torque, fingertip accelerometer spectra and learned external joint torque to output equilibrium pose, stiffness, wrench feedforward and grasp-force setpoints; and a ≥1 kHz hand-designed impedance controller with reference limiting, reference spreading across the impact and a ≥200 Hz grip-force loop. It should be built from openpi/LeRobot, Reactive Diffusion Policy, the HIL-SERL Franka controllers and Isaac Lab, and validated first on two instrumented tasks: driving a pre-started nail 20 mm in ten or fewer strikes with per-strike tool slip under 0.7 cm and 1.4°, and classifying drill-bit engaged/free-spin/cam-out within one revolution at ≥95% accuracy. Because no dataset, benchmark or simulator captures impact force, tool slip or bit engagement, the first deliverable is the instrumented rig that produces that ground truth.

## No system yet fuses a slow VLA with a kilohertz tactile grip loop for hammering or drilling

The novelty screen scored each candidate on six criteria — a VLA or learned world model as the slow policy (C1), a high-frequency low-level controller (C2), inputs spanning tactile, vision, joint state and joint effort (C3), dynamic regulation of grip force, regrasp and torque (C4), a hammer or power-screw task (C5), and a robot-to-tool-to-target separation (C6) — and the literature splits into three camps that never intersect. The first camp has the *task* but no fast loop. Being-H0.7's latent world-action model lists "Pick up the hammer and drive the nail into the hole" among twelve real tasks, but "the policy relies on RGB-only visual observations" at 10–20 Hz and reports no per-task score, so whether the nail is actually driven is unverified ([Being-H0.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00078v1)). RL Token fine-tunes π0.6 with roughly 5 h of online RL so the robot "must use an electric screwdriver to drive an M3 screw into a threaded receptacle," lifting critical-phase success **from 20% to 65%** at 50 Hz — from up to four RGB images plus joint positions, with no force, torque or tactile channel ([RL Token](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23073)). iTUP pairs GPT-4o tool grounding with grasp selection that minimizes predicted interaction wrench and hammers nails on a UR5e at **76.67%**, but executes open-loop trapezoidal trajectories; its authors note that prior hammering work "never consider[s] the torque incurred during hammer-nail interaction … tool slippage or displacement due to unstable grasps" ([iTUP](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01399v1)).

The second camp has the *architecture* but only quasi-static tasks. FAVLA runs a variable-rate fast action expert under a π0 slow VLM with 200 Hz flange force/torque (F/T) and is explicitly motivated by "delayed responses to impacts, stick-slip, and force spikes," yet its tasks are USB insertion, gear assembly, box flipping and board wiping with peak contact forces of **7.7–9.9 N** — roughly an order of magnitude below hammer loads ([FAVLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23648v1)). Figure's Helix 02 stacks a VLM over a 200 Hz System 1 fed by fingertip tactile sensors and a 10M-parameter System 0 that outputs joint commands at 1 kHz, but its documented contact skills are bottle-cap unscrewing and a syringe plunger, not power tools ([Figure, Helix 02](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix-02)). Opt2VLA chains a 5 Hz VLA to a 200 Hz force-aware RL whole-body controller and a 2 kHz PD loop on the Agility Digit humanoid, tracking contact-force references with 1.7–7.0 N mean error, on surface wiping and box pushing ([Opt2VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.23968)). AT-VLA closes a tactile stream in **0.04 s** at a 3:1 slow/fast ratio and still reports "occasional gripper slippage when grip firmness isn't optimal" on lid unscrewing, because it never commands grip force ([AT-VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07308)). The third camp regulates grip with tactile sensing on tools but has no VLA and no impacts: DexScrew reaches **95% screwdriving progress** with 120-element three-axis fingertip arrays and tactile behavior cloning at 20 Hz on a *manual* screwdriver ([DexScrew](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02011)), and Tactile-VLA is the one VLA that outputs a target contact force and uses gripper width "to control the internal grasping force," on USB insertion and wiping ([Tactile-VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09160)).

Two systematic checks corroborate the table. The 53-method survey of tactile- and force-aware robot learning counts 18 methods with wrist F/T, 9 with joint torque and 27 with tactile sensors, and its full text contains no occurrence of "hammer," "drill" or "nail" ([Shan et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.07558)). On the humanoid side, none of Atlas, Optimus, Figure, Sanctuary, Apptronik, Agility or 1X has published a credible hammering or power-tool demonstration; the Atlas/TRI Large Behavior Model consumes stereo images and proprioception at 30 Hz with 48-step chunks and no tactile input ([Boston Dynamics](https://bostondynamics.com/blog/large-behavior-models-atlas-find-new-footing/)), UBTech merely states that Walker S "can use an electric screwdriver to tighten screws on car doors" without disclosing autonomy ([UBTech](https://www.ubtrobot.com/en/humanoid/solutions/industry)), and at the August 2026 World Humanoid Robot Games nail-hammering event "many nails were driven at angles" and the referee observed that "the initial strike to secure the nail is crucial" ([Bastille Post](https://www.bastillepost.com/global/article/6104693-robots-compete-to-hammer-nails-with-precision-at-humanoid-robot-games-in-beijing)) — direct evidence that seating and strike-axis control, not swing power, are the current failure modes.

## Every hammering demo treats the hammer as a rigid link and never controls the strike

The real-hardware hammering lineage spans three approaches and shares one blind spot. Vision-only learned policies began with TOG-Net, which predicted open-loop motion-primitive parameters from a depth image and reached **80.0% hammering** on a Sawyer with failures "usually caused by occasional deviations during the execution" ([TOG-Net](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.09266)); PoCo composes RGB, point-cloud and one GelSight-finger policies at 5–10 Hz for "pressing a pin downward by 2 centimeters using a hammer tool" and concedes such policies "can overfit and perform poorly in tasks requiring delicate contact control" ([PoCo](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02511v3)); TeleDexter's behavior-cloned HammerDriver policy reaches 73.3% at 30 Hz on a LEAP or Sharpa hand and attributes failures to "tracking stall" from the absence of tactile sensing ([TeleDexter](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.11481v1)). Model-based control is represented by Ti et al., whose Franka holds a 3D-printed hammer between "two pieces of sponge" and infers impact force only from nail insertion depth, because a hammer-mounted sensor "would increase the weight … and cause a bias for the gravity center, further increasing the grip instability" ([Ti et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05502)). Sim-to-real RL is represented by SimToolReal, whose 22-DoF Sharpa hand swings a hammer as a free-space pose sequence and whose authors warn it "does not guarantee functional task completion, especially for high-force interactions" ([SimToolReal](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16863)), and by a roofing humanoid whose "hammering" and "nailgun" tasks drive no nails and involve "no force or tactile feedback" ([roofing G1](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20558)). Grasp-to-Act is the sole work that adapts the grasp *during* strikes, and it does so at 30 Hz from finger positions, targets and torques alone while the arm trajectory is replayed open-loop ([Grasp-to-Act](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20466)).

Impact-aware control has matured in parallel without ever touching a tool. The EU I.AM. project's reference-spreading (RS) stack runs a QP torque controller at 1 kHz on dual Franka arms with a momentum observer for impact detection over a 200 ms window, achieving **90% grasp success** on 0.64–2.12 kg boxes versus 80% without RS and 40% without velocity feedback, and is "the first experimental evaluation of RS control on a robotic setup" ([van Steen et al., T-RO 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.08643)). Its companion sim-to-real study, using the nonsmooth AGX engine and a 4 kHz torque loop, matched post-impact velocity jumps to **3.1% average error** but warns that "feedback control acts while impact-induced vibrations are still active, so evaluating impacts from raw velocity signals is unreliable" ([van Steen et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06319v1)); the earlier ICRA 2021 study found rigid models predict "no oscillations" where the real arm shows a "damped oscillatory response after the impact" ([Aouaj et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08220)). Every I.AM. impact is a box hit at 0.3–0.6 m/s, and the interim partial-contact mode "may require manual tuning for different types of object" ([time-invariant RS](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.09870v1)). Stouraitis et al. showed that co-optimizing stiffness with contact timing lets a KUKA LWR at 200 Hz halt a 20 kg object travelling at ~0.66–0.88 m/s with under 30 N of force ([Stouraitis et al., IROS 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.13374)), and the July 2026 nonsmooth-impact-direction paper explicitly names "repetitive hammering" as a *future* application ([Kirner & Ott](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13768)). The lesson for this project is that the controller-level machinery for a single impact exists, but it has never been stressed by a hand-held tool with its own grasp compliance, by strike velocities above 0.6 m/s, or by repeated percussion.

Drilling and screwdriving research divides into industrial process monitoring and dexterous-hand skill learning, and neither closes a learned loop on engagement. The discriminative signals are consistent: a human study found "people apply axial force to the screws to avoid screwdriver slippage (cam-outs)," that "this axial force is proportional to the torque which is required for screwing," and that cam-outs "correspond to sharp drops in torque," a strategy the RecyBot unscrewing robot copied by raising axial force whenever slip was detected ([Mironov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10386)). A CNN on driver torque alone detects stripped drives "within less than one revolution" at **0.98 accuracy** ([J. Manufacturing Systems 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278612522001248)), autoencoders on F/T reach AUROC above 0.96 for screwing anomalies ([Grambow et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26308)), and PyScrew publishes 34,000+ industrial screw-driving operations with up to 27 labeled error types ([PyScrew](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11925)). The cautionary result is AURSAD: with UR3e and screwdriver telemetry sampled at only 100 Hz, a raw-data LSTM scored **F1 = 4.79%** and only feature engineering rescued performance ([AURSAD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.01409)). On the learning side, a Shadow Hand turning a screwdriver on a WAM succeeded in 17 of 20 trials but failed when "the screwdriver's handle slipped off the fingers or its tip slid out of the screw's drive," and its authors had to replace the screw–substrate interaction with a one-DoF resistance model because "MuJoCo cannot simulate the complex interactions between the screw and the substrate" ([Tang, Jia, Xue, ICRA 2024](https://www.cs.iastate.edu/files/inline-files/icra24.pdf)). Powered tools remain the province of scripted systems: at the DRC Finals several teams "dropped the drill or the drill shut off" and MIT's Atlas saw "the bit slipped out of the wall during the cut" ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/drc-finals-course)), HRP-5P screwed drywall to a wall with an AR-marker-localized tool in 2018 ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2018-11-humanoid-robot-prototype-hrp-5p-capable.html)), and the only learned tactile-hand drill result, VisTacAlign, merely activates the trigger and lifts the drill ([VisTacAlign](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30959)). Nothing found detects drill-bit engagement, cam-out or bit slip from hand-mounted sensors, and no simulator models bit-recess engagement.

## Force-aware VLAs tokenize force at 10 Hz and leave regulation to position controllers

The tactile and force VLA wave of 2025–2026 is large but architecturally uniform. Almost every model — TLA, VTLA, OmniVTLA, ForceVLA, TacVLA, TaF-VLA, TA-VLA, TAP-VLA, T-Rex, VTAM, Dream-Tac, VT-WAM, FM-VLA, TacForcing, MoSS, VLA-Touch — compresses tactile or force into tokens, gates them into a π0/π0.5/RDT/GO-1 backbone and still emits **position-only action chunks at roughly 8–11 Hz** where the rate is stated ([TA-VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07962); [VLA-Touch](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17294v1)). ForceVLA fuses 6-axis F/T through a mixture of experts for +23.2% average success but has "positional outputs only; no explicit force/impedance commands" ([ForceVLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22159v1)), and TaF-VLA, after pretraining on more than 10 million tactile–force frames, still names its receding-horizon output as the limit on "high-frequency dynamic events" ([TaF-VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20321)). T-Rex is the only VLA that ingests tactile at 300 Hz, and it does so by VQ-encoding 16-sample force histories for a 30 Hz policy — the bandwidth serves perception, not control ([T-Rex](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17055)). The exceptions that put a force-domain quantity in the *action space* are few: Tactile-VLA outputs target contact force and regulates internal grasp force via gripper width, producing 0.51 N versus 2.57 N grasps for "softly" versus "hard" instructions with 100 Hz tactile and 20 Hz vision ([Tactile-VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09160)); Tabero-VTLA emits decoupled force–position commands to a fixed hybrid controller and cuts grip force by more than 70% under "gentle" instructions ([Tabero-VTLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27886)); and "Compliance for Free" fine-tunes a VLA to output per-axis stiffness alongside pose and is the only one of five policies whose contact force responds to a "firm" instruction (6.4 N → 9.1 N RMS) ([Compliance for Free](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19976)).

The force-output design is much better developed in non-VLA imitation learning, and it is where the execution rates the user needs already appear. TacDiffusion generates 6-D wrenches at **50–500 Hz** (141.8 Hz for the deployed model) from an 18-D wrench/velocity input with no vision, bridges to a 1 kHz force-impedance controller through a second-order dynamic-system filter, and achieves 95.7% zero-shot success on sub-millimeter insertions ([TacDiffusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11047v1)). The Adaptive Compliance Policy outputs, per arm, a 9-D reference pose, a 9-D virtual target and a scalar stiffness that a **500 Hz admittance controller** tracks using 7 kHz ATI F/T, and beats fixed-compliant and stiff baselines 96% to 23% and 14% on item flipping ([ACP](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.09309)); UMI-FT extends that action space with a grasp-force output executed by a 30 Hz gripper force loop, using per-finger 6-axis CoinFT sensors at 360 Hz that capture "both external contact forces and internal grasp force" ([UMI-FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09988)). Imp-ACT emits equilibrium pose plus directional stiffness at 50 Hz over a 1 kHz Franka loop and reduces wiping vibration **29× versus a compliant and 180× versus a stiff baseline**, while recommending a passivity filter "to regulate energy injection associated with stiffness variations" ([Imp-ACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.31225)). FARM predicts target grip force with a 7 Hz diffusion policy and a 25 Hz PID and reaches 95–100% on screw tightening ([FARM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13324v1)). Two negative results shape the interface choice: ForceMimic found that force-as-*input* baselines "struggled when interactive forces during execution deviated significantly from training data," so force is more robust as a target handed to a controller than as a raw observation ([ForceMimic](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07554v1)), and Bogdanovic et al. found direct torque output "has difficulty to robustly solve the problem without any given structure in the action space," which is why impedance became the preferred RL action space ([Bogdanovic et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.07500)).

Two further findings govern how force should enter a flow-matching slow layer. FACT diagnosed why π0-style policies fail in contact: "delta collapse," where actions shrink toward zero on contact, compounded by a Beta noise schedule that gives only 8.9% of gradient signal to the fine-correction regime; fixing the schedule, modulating by current force, adding a 2 s force-history token stream and gating gradients in non-contact phases lifted average success **from 39.0% to 66.0%** where ForceVLA managed 40.5% ([FACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.01402)). "Tune to Learn" showed that imitation policies work best with compliant, overdamped low-level gains and 10–20 Hz policy rates because stiff gains produce "high-frequency oscillations" on real hardware — the controller gains are an inductive bias, not a tuning afterthought ([Tune to Learn](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02523)). Joint effort as an input follows the same pattern: TA-VLA's motor-current torque token raises contact-rich success from 0–5/20 to 15–18/20 but warns of "motor calibration, sensor noise, or thermal drift" ([TA-VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07962)), and FACTR 2's NEXT estimator, trained in one minute on ten minutes of free motion and run at 100 Hz, beats raw current on NIST insertion (0.567 vs 0.431) and cap screwing (0.525 vs 0.440) while explicitly including "no hammering or impact tasks" ([FACTR 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12406v1)). The survey's caveat stands: "Raw joint torque includes loads caused by both robot motion and external contact" and is embodiment-dependent ([Shan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.07558)).

Learned world models cannot occupy the fast loop today. V-JEPA 2-AC predicts at 4 fps and its CEM planner takes **16 seconds per action** ([V-JEPA 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09985)); the 2026 visuo-tactile world models run at 6 fps (VT-WM, FeelWorld), 15 Hz (ViTacWorld) or at most 60 Hz for OmniVTA's reflexive tactile controller, and all represent contact as deformation or slip rather than force — FeelWorld states outright that CEM "is not suitable for high-frequency real-time control" ([FeelWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24267); [OmniVTA](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19201v1)), TacForeSight conditions on 120 Hz F/T but "does not predict future force values" ([TacForeSight](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.11184)), and NVIDIA reports world-action models cost 590–800 ms per chunk against ~190 ms for π0.5 ([NVIDIA WAM blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/pretrained-to-imagine-fine-tuned-to-act-the-rise-of-world-action-models/)). Even sampling-based MPC over a GPU physics model replans at only 8–10 Hz on a real Franka because "small timesteps are required to avoid deep interpenetrations" ([Dierking et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.20712v1)). The demonstrated value of world models for a project like this is offline: ViTacWorld's generated "dream data" lifted a policy from 42.5% to 80% over two augmentation rounds ([ViTacWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22530v1)), and no world model of any kind has a time step below ~16 ms or any representation of impacts or tool vibration.

## Physics and physiology fix the timing budget: 3 ms pulses, 150 ms anticipation, 250 ms VLA latency

The events the controller must handle are fast and broadband. A textbook hammer–nail contact for a 0.45 kg head at 7 m/s lasts about **2.86 ms** ([College Physics Answers](https://collegephysicsanswers.com/openstax-solutions/0450-kg-hammer-moving-horizontally-700-ms-when-it-strikes-nail-and-comes-rest)), riveting hammers produce unweighted handle accelerations averaging 68.81 m/s² in the lab with spectra measured across 6.3–1250 Hz ([PMC4677776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4677776/)), electric demolition hammers concentrate energy near their ~50 Hz blow rate with bit-dependent content at 50–200 Hz ([J. Low Frequency Noise 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14613484231186769)), and hammer-drill impact rates fall between 10 and 100 Hz ([US 10906166](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10906166)). The only robot-measured hammering impact force is ARMADA's 50.5 N mean, which its authors put roughly 10× below a human palm strike ([ARMADA](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16908)); sensorized hammerstone strikes by humans peak at **135–730 N**, sampled at 100 kHz ([PMC11407656](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11407656/)). Grip and feed forces for powered impact tools are large and palm-dominated: fifteen professional operators held a hammer drill's main handle with a median grip of **84–156 N** while pushing with 100–200 N, and higher grip reduced transmitted vibration ([Lindenmann et al., Applied Ergonomics 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003687021000776)); doubling hammer mass from 1 lb to 2 lb raised shoulder, elbow and wrist moments 50–150%, most in the cocking and strike/follow-through phases ([Balendra & Langenderfer 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003687016302617)). These numbers place the problem in the power-grasp regime, well above the 20 N fingertip force of a Sharpa Wave hand ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2026/06/02/sharpa-wave-high-end-dexterous-robotic-hand-with-22-dof-high-sensitivity-dynamic-tactile-array/)), which argues for palm-level sensing and a parallel or power gripper before any dexterous hand.

Sensor bandwidth follows directly. Resolving the *shape* of a 3 ms pulse needs 1–2 kHz of usable bandwidth; detecting its *occurrence* is easier because the structure rings for tens of milliseconds afterward, and MicCheck notes a "knock" "tends to excite audible resonance" ([MicCheck](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18299)); resolving drill fundamentals plus harmonics needs several hundred hertz to a kilohertz. The decoding recipe for kilohertz channels is settled: high-pass around 10–30 Hz, log-mel or short-window FFT with 5–16 ms hops, then a small CNN or an AudioSet-pretrained spectrogram transformer. ManiWAV's 48 kHz contact microphone lifted flipping from 25% to 90% and pouring from 0% to 90% ([ManiWAV](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19464)); SpectRobot renders 0–100 kHz vibration into 224×224 spectrogram images consumed by an ordinary vision encoder, finds that 2.9 s of context beats 0.3 s (86–92% vs 31%) while bandwidth above 10 kHz adds nothing, and gets 82% from a MEMS accelerometer on a ~100 € Teensy rig ([SpectRobot](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24621v1)); Taunyazov et al. localized impacts to under 1 cm on a 20 cm hand-held rod purely from vibration transmitted through the tool ([Extended Tactile Perception](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00489)). Closed-loop slip work quantifies achievable latencies: PapillArray's GRU detects incipient slip within 10 ms at 96.8% online success ([PapillArray](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.04011)); SlipSense infers in 2.3 ms, catches 76% of slips within 23.1 ms, but still needs ~50 ms from detection to peak gripper force ([SlipSense](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.15910)); a tendon-driven hand detects slip in 20.4 ± 6 ms yet measures **185 ± 27 ms** from reaction to slip-stop ([PzE hand](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16127)). None of these detectors has been characterized under impulsive loads, and impact-induced vibration is spectrally similar to slip — an open false-positive risk.

Human physiology confirms that the strike itself cannot be regulated reactively and shows what the anticipatory profile must look like. Because collision loads rise "in less than 20 ms … only predictive control mechanisms can compensate," and self-produced collisions are gripped more efficiently and improve with repetition while imposed ones do not ([Delevoye-Turrell, Li & Wing 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/02724980245000025)). Johansson and Westling found preparatory grip increase and "general stiffening of the hand/arm system" beginning "ca. 150 ms prior to the impact," scaled to ball weight, drop height and friction, while impact-triggered reflexes arrived "too late to prevent slips" but restored the safety margin ([Johansson & Westling 1988](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00247523)); the slip-triggered grip upgrade itself takes **74 ± 9 ms** ([Johansson & Westling 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3582528)), and load-pulse responses appear "from 60 to 90 ms after onset" ([Cole & Abbs 1988](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/10.1152/jn.1988.60.4.1513)). In targeted tapping, "maximum grip force occurred approximately 65 milliseconds after peak load at contact" with a stereotyped, pre-programmed profile, and the authors conclude that grip force regulates both stiffness and damping of the hand ([White et al. 2011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306452211004957)); in robot-simulated collisions the grip-force onset and peak timing did not depend on load, indicating "time-locked planning of the complete grip force profile" with only magnitude and rate adjusted ([Kuling et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00221-019-05606-y)). Grip–load coupling is a forward model that holds for inertial, viscous and elastic loads ([Flanagan & Wing 1997](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/17/4/1519)), and the dominant hand shows "stronger grip-load coupling, indicating superior predictive control" while the non-dominant hand "stabilized the object better and exerted a higher grip force" — a natural template for a tool hand and a nail-holding hand ([Naik & Ambike 2025](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=hammering%20AND%20%22grip%20force%22&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=8)). For drilling, humans sense engagement through the tool: probe-mediated roughness "is coded temporally by vibrations that are transmitted through the probe" with a 0.92 correlation to vibratory power ([Yoshioka et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17558923)), and even expert surgeons take **0.226 ± 0.181 s** to react to drill breakthrough with 3.5 mm overshoot, versus 0.020 s and 0.54 mm with automated haptic detection ([PMC9837054](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9837054/)).

Against these budgets the learned layers are slow. A π0.5 chunk costs 76 ms to infer on a datacenter GPU, plus 10–20 ms network and queue position, for a baseline reaction delay of about six 20 ms steps ([RTC](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07339)); on Jetson Thor π0 runs at 19 Hz (52.57 ms), below typical camera rates ([VLA-Perf](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18397v1)); Gemini Robotics quotes ~250 ms end-to-end ([Gemini Robotics](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20020v1)). The "50 Hz" of these systems is chunk execution, not sensing-to-action bandwidth. Simulators are equally limited at the impact end: MuJoCo drops strict complementarity for soft contacts at a default 2 ms step ([MuJoCo docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/computation/index.html)), Drake's hydroelastic model "does not introduce state" and misses "short time scale waves" ([Drake](https://drake.mit.edu/doxygen_cxx/group__hydroelastic__user__guide.html)), and NVIDIA Factory's ISO-thread SDF contacts run at 1/60 s with one substep and were never validated on a real robot ([Factory](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.03532)); Isaac Lab ships "no dedicated tool-based environments (hammer, drill, screwdriver)" ([Isaac Lab](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/overview/environments.html)). The workarounds with hardware evidence are velocity-dependent restitution plus a residual contact network, which cut Sony's table-tennis landing error 59% ([Conti et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.28805)); learning force *direction* in simulation while tuning force *magnitude* "with a single scalar per contact state" on hardware ([Direction Matters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14174)); and adding real tactile behavior cloning after sim RL, which took DexScrew from 41.6% direct transfer to 95% ([DexScrew](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02011)).

## The novelty is closing the loop through the tool, not the two degrees of separation

Several parts of the proposal are not new, and saying so sharpens what is. The slow/fast split is now industry standard (Helix, GR00T, RDP, FAVLA, AT-VLA), force-domain action spaces exist (Tactile-VLA, ACP/UMI-FT, TacDiffusion, Imp-ACT), impedance scheduling for impacts is demonstrated (Stouraitis, IMA-Catcher, reference spreading), vibration sensing through a grasped tool dates to 2021 ([Taunyazov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00489)), and every hammering neighbor already acts on a target one object removed from the hand. The "two degrees of separation" framing is therefore a task property, not a contribution.

Five things have no precedent in any source retrieved. First, a learned loop that *regulates* grip force, stiffness or applied torque during percussive or vibratory tool use: every learned grip-force regulator found runs at 12–30 Hz (FARM 25 Hz, UMI-FT 30 Hz, TactileReflex ~12 Hz) and all were validated on lifting, pouring or tilting, never under impact ([FARM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13324v1); [UMI-FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09988); [TactileReflex](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23568)). Second, joint effort as a first-class input to the *fast* loop for impact and engagement: RDP lists joint-torque sensors only as optional, TA-VLA folds a 2 s torque history into a single decoder token at ~11 Hz, FACTR 2 stops at 100 Hz with no impact tasks, and no work uses joint effort to detect or respond to an impact ([RDP repo](https://github.com/xiaoxiaoxh/reactive_diffusion_policy); [TA-VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07962); [FACTR 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12406v1)). Third, engagement, cam-out and bit-slip estimation *inside* a policy that drives grip and axial force: these exist only as offline anomaly classifiers on driver torque or F/T ([Grambow et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26308); [J. Manufacturing Systems 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278612522001248)), and the RecyBot axial-force response was a threshold rule, not a learned policy ([Mironov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10386)). Fourth, an online estimate of the tool–target coupling — nail penetration resistance, thread engagement, hammer face squareness — as a control state; body-schema work identifies tool inertia offline by motor babbling, and no work models "the dynamics of the tool–target pair" online ([Frontiers body-schema survey](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2022.1009488/xml)). Fifth, a grip profile structured on the human impact template — predictive ramp ≥150 ms before contact, time-locked peak ~60 ms after peak load, reflex-driven margin update for the *next* strike — which no robot system implements. The absence of any dataset, benchmark or simulator with impact-force, tool-slip or engagement ground truth means the evaluation infrastructure itself is a contribution: existing hammer benchmarks (Meta-World, Adroit with its 15 N nail friction, RoboTwin, HumanoidToolBench) score only nail displacement in soft-contact simulators ([Adroit hammer](https://robotics.farama.org/envs/adroit_hand/adroit_hammer/); [Meta-World](https://metaworld.farama.org/benchmark/task_descriptions/)), FurnitureBench's real screwing is gripper-rotated at 10 Hz ([FurnitureBench](https://clvrai.github.io/furniture-bench/)), and no human tool-use corpus records force during hammering or power-tool use — EgoDex's 829 hours are pose-only and EgoTactile's 162-sensor glove samples at ~17 Hz and contains screwdrivers but no hammers or drills ([EgoDex](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11709v3); [EgoTactile](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09243)).

The honest counterpoint is that the novelty is integrative rather than algorithmic: each ingredient exists on hardware separately, and the FAVLA, Opt2VLA and LIFT papers already argue the thesis that high-frequency force must bypass the slow VLM ([FAVLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23648v1); [LIFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14236)). What makes the integration scientific rather than engineering is the regime change: post-impact ringing corrupts the very effort signals a regulator would use ([van Steen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06319v1)), slip detectors have never been tested against impact vibration, reference spreading has never been run through a compliant grasp on a swung tool, and grip forces of 100–150 N sit outside every tactile VLA's operating envelope. Those are open empirical questions, and answering them on a well-instrumented rig is the project.

## Recommended method: a three-layer stack with force-domain interfaces and a force-truth rig

The decisive choice is to make the VLA the slow planner, keep any video or latent world model *off* the critical path, and spend the "world model" budget on a small learned tool–target state estimator instead. The evidence for the three-tier structure is uniform across Helix 02, SERL/HIL-SERL, TacDiffusion, ACP and Opt2VLA; the evidence against putting learning in the kilohertz layer is that only Helix's S0, trained by sim-to-real RL across 200,000 environments, has ever done it, and hammering physics is exactly what simulators reproduce worst ([Helix 02](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix-02)).

The action space at the L2→L1 boundary should be pose plus stiffness plus wrench feedforward plus grasp force, not joint torques or pure positions: ACP's stiffness output beat stiff and compliant baselines by 73 and 82 points, HIL-SERL had to switch from pose targets to feedforward wrenches to whip a Jenga block and flip a pan, and Bogdanovic's torque-output RL failed without structure ([ACP](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.09309); [HIL-SERL](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21845); [Bogdanovic et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.07500)). Grip force is an impedance-shaping command in its own right, as White et al. showed for humans, so the pre-impact grasp-force setpoint should follow the human profile: ramp beginning at least 150 ms before predicted contact, scaled to tool mass, planned strike velocity and estimated friction, peaking 50–70 ms after peak load, with slip and vibration feedback raising the margin for the next strike rather than trying to rescue the current one ([White et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306452211004957); [Johansson & Westling 1988](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00247523)).

### Sensing choices with bandwidth justification

A wrist 6-axis F/T sensor sampled at ≥1 kHz is mandatory and is the single most valuable channel: Bota SensONE streams at 3840 Hz over EtherCAT with an integrated IMU and ATI Net F/T at 7 kHz over UDP, both comfortably resolving a 3 ms pulse and the reaction torque of a drill ([Bota](https://www.botasys.com/force-torque-sensors); [ATI](https://www.ati-ia.com/products/ft/ft_NetFT.aspx)). A three-axis MEMS accelerometer in or under the grip pad sampled at 8 kHz, with a range of at least ±16 g because riveting-hammer handles alone see 25–70 m/s² unweighted and hand hammers more ([SlipSense](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.15910); [PMC4677776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4677776/)), supplies the vibration spectra for engagement and slip; a piezo contact microphone at 48 kHz is a cheap redundant channel with proven decoders ([ManiWAV](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19464)). A pressure array at ≥200 Hz across the pads (Xela uSkin at 500 Hz, PapillArray at 1 kHz) gives grip-force distribution and center of pressure, which is what a power grasp needs; camera-based gels at 25–60 fps alias every signal of interest and should be reserved, if used at all, for the slow layer ([Xela](https://xelarobotics.com/technology/); [PapillArray](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.04011)). Joint effort should come from a torque-sensed arm at 1 kHz (Franka's 13-bit joint torque sensors, or Kinova Gen3) and be converted to *external* torque with a momentum observer or a NEXT-style learned free-motion model, since raw current forces the policy to disentangle actuator dynamics from contact ([PMC7435754](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7435754/); [FACTR 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12406v1)). The platform recommendation is a Franka FR3 with a parallel gripper whose force loop runs at ≥200 Hz and whose pads are compliant (the sponge or rubber interface used by Ti et al. and Torobo is what allowed their hammers to survive), because the FR3 already has 1 kHz torque control, open impedance controllers, and precedents in RS, TacDiffusion, Imp-ACT and HIL-SERL; a dexterous hand should wait until the grip-force envelope (≥100 N sustained) is met.

Human demonstration data should be collected through an instrumented tool handle rather than a glove, because the loads are palm-dominated and existing gloves sample at 17–30 Hz; the handle should carry 6-axis F/T plus an IMU at ≥1 kHz and share its pad layout between human and robot, following OSMO's shared-layout logic ([OSMO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08920)). Every existing handheld device downsamples force to ≤30 Hz before training; this project must keep the kilohertz stream intact for the L2 detectors and compress it only for the VLA.

### Minimal first task and measurable success criteria

Task A is nail driving with a pre-started nail in a soft pine or foam board, which removes the seating problem the Beijing games exposed and matches the pilot-hole setup of Ti et al. The performance target is driving the nail **20 mm in ten or fewer strikes**, which ARMADA achieves at 10 ± 1.26 strikes with a 50.5 N mean impact and which beats the human average of 13.15 ± 6.41 in that study ([ARMADA](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16908)). Grasp-stability targets are per-strike translational slip ≤0.7 cm and rotational slip ≤1.4°, the level Grasp-to-Act reached with proprioception alone, and zero drops over 100 consecutive strikes ([Grasp-to-Act](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20466)). Control-quality targets are strike-on-nail ≥90%, impact detection from F/T within 10 ms of onset, a grip ramp beginning ≥150 ms before predicted contact and peaking 50–70 ms after peak load, joint-velocity feedback gated for the first 50 ms of ringing, and peak joint torque no higher than a stiff position-controlled baseline. Task B is drill-engagement detection and regulation with a cordless driver and a #8 wood screw in pine: classify {free-spin, engaged, cam-out, seated} at ≥95% accuracy within one revolution of the event, raise axial push on cam-out detection as humans do, and reach ≥90% fully seated first attempts over 50 trials with at least a 50% reduction in cam-out count versus a fixed-force baseline ([Mironov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10386)). Both tasks are one arm, one tool, one target, and both produce force-truth logs that no benchmark currently offers; the VLA is added only after L1 + L2 clear these bars, mirroring the finding that FoAR's 10 Hz policy over a 100 Hz controller already chops peppers at 70% without any force regulation of the impact ([FoAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15753v1)).

### Open-source stacks to build from

The slow layer should come from openpi (Apache-2.0; π0, π0-FAST, π0.5 with fine-tuning) or LeRobot (Apache-2.0; π0.5, SmolVLA, GR00T N1.7, HIL-SERL, and an asynchronous PolicyServer/RobotClient with tunable chunk thresholds), noting that neither exposes a force, torque or tactile observation key, so the integrator must add them ([openpi](https://github.com/Physical-Intelligence/openpi); [LeRobot](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot); [LeRobot async](https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/async)). Reactive Diffusion Policy (MIT) is the only open code that already implements a slow latent policy with a fast tactile decoder and optional joint-torque input, on Flexiv and Franka Research 3 ([RDP repo](https://github.com/xiaoxiaoxh/reactive_diffusion_policy)). HIL-SERL (Apache-2.0) supplies the 1 kHz impedance-based serl_franka_controllers and the real-world RL loop ([hil-serl](https://github.com/rail-berkeley/hil-serl)). Isaac Lab (BSD-3) with TacSL's visuo-tactile sensor and the Factory nut-thread environments covers simulation, with Newton (Apache-2.0) and MuJoCo MPC (Apache-2.0) as alternatives for contact physics and swing-trajectory baselines ([Isaac Lab](https://github.com/isaac-sim/IsaacLab); [Newton](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton); [MuJoCo MPC](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_mpc)). Meta's Sparsh-X multisensory encoder (image, audio, IMU, pressure) is the closest vibration-aware pretrained tactile encoder but is CC BY-NC 4.0, so it is research-only ([Sparsh license](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/facebookresearch/sparsh/main/LICENSE.md); [sparsh-multisensory-touch](https://github.com/facebookresearch/sparsh-multisensory-touch)). TA-VLA's released code shows how to inject torque tokens into a π0 decoder ([TA-VLA GitHub](https://github.com/ZZongzheng0918/TA-VLA)).

### Main risks and mitigations

| Risk | Evidence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Post-impact ringing corrupts velocity and effort feedback for tens of ms, destabilizing any regulator that trusts them | Rigid models show no oscillation while real arms ring ([Aouaj et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08220)); RS authors warn raw velocity evaluation is unreliable ([van Steen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06319v1)) | RS ante/post references with interim mode; gate velocity feedback for ~50 ms after detected impact; use F/T and accelerometer, not joint velocity, for post-impact state |
| Slip detectors false-trigger on impact vibration | No slip detector has been tested under impulsive loads; spectra overlap ([SlipSense](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.15910)) | Phase-gated detectors trained on rig data with impact negatives; contact gating as in TacVLA and AT-VLA ([TacVLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12665v1)) |
| Actuation latency exceeds the strike: 50–185 ms from detection to grip change | SlipSense ~50 ms to peak force; tendon hand 185 ± 27 ms ([PzE hand](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16127)) | Set grip pre-impact from predicted load (human 150 ms lead); use feedback only to update the next strike's margin; choose a gripper with a ≥200 Hz force loop |
| Sim-to-real gap for impacts: velocity jumps transfer, ringing, micro-slip and penetration resistance do not | 3.1% velocity-jump error but no ringing ([van Steen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06319v1)); DexScrew 41.6% direct vs 95% after real tactile BC ([DexScrew](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02011)) | Learn kinematics and force direction in sim, magnitudes and grip schedule on hardware; residual contact model; velocity-dependent restitution |
| Variable stiffness injects energy and oscillates | Imp-ACT calls for a passivity filter ([Imp-ACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.31225)); stiff gains cause high-frequency oscillation ([Tune to Learn](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02523)) | Passivity constraint on stiffness rate; compliant, overdamped default gains; reference limiting |
| Flow-matching slow policy collapses actions on contact | Delta collapse and 8.9% gradient in the fine regime ([FACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.01402)) | Logit-normal schedule, force-history tokens, gradient gating; keep force regulation in L2 not L3 |
| Force-as-observation distribution shift; raw current drifts | Force-input baselines underperformed ([ForceMimic](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07554v1)); current-based torque suffers calibration and thermal drift ([TA-VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07962)) | Force enters L3 as targets and compressed tokens; L2 consumes learned external torque, not raw current |
| Tool rotates or shifts in the gripper; grip forces exceed hand capacity | Hammer rotation in sponge grip ([Ti et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05502)); 84–156 N grip on power tools ([Lindenmann et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003687021000776)) | Parallel/power gripper with compliant high-friction pads and palm pressure sensing first; regrasp as an L3 decision between strikes; dexterous hand later |
| Powered-tool safety: drops, shut-offs, bit walk, torque saturation stops | DRC drill failures ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/drc-finals-course)); FAVLA force-saturation stops ([FAVLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23648v1)) | Clutch-limited driver, reference limiting, tethered E-stop, pre-started fasteners in the first task |
| No dataset or benchmark exists | Every hammer benchmark is soft-contact sim scoring displacement ([Adroit](https://robotics.farama.org/envs/adroit_hand/adroit_hammer/)) | Build the force-truth rig first and publish it; auto-label from tool-side sensors |
| VLA edge latency on-robot | π0 at 19 Hz on Jetson Thor ([VLA-Perf](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18397v1)) | Asynchronous chunking; VLA never on the reactive path; L1 and L2 run on the real-time host |

## Conclusion

The field has quietly agreed with the user's thesis — FAVLA, Opt2VLA, FACT and LIFT all argue that high-frequency force must bypass the slow VLM — but has tested it only where peak forces are around 10 N and nothing happens faster than a few hundred milliseconds. Hammering and drilling change the regime rather than the architecture: the decisive quantities become the 150 ms predictive grip lead, the 3 ms pulse, the tens of milliseconds of structural ringing, and the 100–150 N power grasp, none of which any published learned system has confronted. That reframing matters for where to spend effort. The learned components that will earn their keep are the mid-rate estimators — impact phase, slip under vibration, bit engagement, remaining nail depth — because they convert kilohertz signals no VLA can see into state a 1–10 Hz planner can act on, while the kilohertz layer itself should be engineered from impact-aware control principles that already exist and have never been pointed at a tool.

The most valuable near-term artifact is therefore not a model but a measurement: an instrumented hammer-and-drill rig that logs impact force, tool slip and engagement state at 1 kHz alongside vision, tactile and joint effort. It would be the first dataset of its kind, it makes every downstream claim falsifiable, and it converts a seemingly integrative project into one that answers open questions — whether reference spreading survives a compliant grasp on a swung tool, whether slip detectors can be made impact-blind, and whether a predictive grip schedule can match human strike economy. If those three answers come back positive on a single arm with a parallel gripper, the humanoid and dexterous-hand extensions become engineering; if they do not, the field learns something it currently does not know.
