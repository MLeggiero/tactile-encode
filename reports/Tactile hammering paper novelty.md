# Predict the Blow: Where Hammering Grip Control Is New

The project is publishable, and its strongest claim is narrower than the full stack suggests. Many robots hammer nails, but no paper found controls a hammer *held in a grasp that can slip*: one that measures impact force and in-grasp slip, schedules grip force ahead of the blow, and applies impact-aware kilohertz control through that grasp. Under a six-point rubric (real hardware, held tool, nail-depth metric, impact force, grip/slip control, kHz impact-aware control), no prior work scores above 2-3. The closest are Ti et al.'s velocity-controlled Franka planner, which "could not record impact forces", and Grasp-to-Act's 30 Hz proprioceptive slip residual on a LEAP hand ([Ti et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05502); [Grasp-to-Act](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20466)). Five things are genuinely new. The first is the **anticipatory, human-templated grip ramp sized to a predicted impulse**; no robotics paper does this. The second is **reference spreading, momentum-observer detection and feedback gating applied to a tool held through a compliant grasp**, where all prior work impacts with the robot's own end effector. The third is **slip-detector false-positive rates measured under hammer impacts**; no detector has been tested against that negative class. The fourth is the **actuator-feasibility result**: rated dexterous finger torques cannot hold a blow, and self-locking drives see 20-40x their rating. The fifth is a **force-truth hammering dataset**, which does not exist. Four parts are not new and should be presented as integration: the slow VLA over a fast tactile layer (RDP, FAVLA, T-Rex, AT-VLA, Helix 02), learned stiffness-plus-grip action spaces (ACP, UMI-FT, Imp-ACT), the kHz pressure-plus-accelerometer sensing (SlipSense), and the reference-spreading machinery itself (van Steen et al.). The best framing leads with "predict the blow, don't react to it". It presents the grip-impact coupling as the scientific contribution, demotes the VLA to an interface, and splits the work across an RSS 2027 hypothesis-first paper (extended abstract due **December 4, 2026**), an RA-L feasibility paper on dexterous hands, and a later data paper. The user's unifying idea is a phase-indexed, load-predictive grip-and-impedance controller covering impulses, jerks and oscillations. It is a sound organizing principle with real human grounding. As a unified *method* it is only moderately novel: IMPACT already learns internal-model feedforward wrench, and Grasp-to-Act already handles hammering and sawing with one controller. It earns a long journal paper once a second, oscillatory task is on the rig. It should not carry the first conference paper.

## No prior work controls a hammer through a slipping grasp

The hammering literature splits into systems that strike but do not sense the grasp, and systems that sense the grasp but do not model the strike. On the model-based side, Ti, Gao, Zhao and Calinon optimise pre-strike posture for directional velocity manipulability on a real Franka under a **joint-velocity controller**, with no torque or impact model. They pad the gripper with "two pieces of sponge", drive a nail into **plastic foam with a pilot hole** to spare the robot, and state that "we could not record impact forces accurately" because the soft grip keeps force from reaching the robot's sensors, so they infer peak force from insertion depth ([Ti et al., arXiv 2402.05502](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05502)). ARMADA, a $6,100 back-drivable arm, drives a nail 20 mm in **10 ± 1.26 strikes** against 13.15 ± 6.41 for 14 humans. It uses pre-computed swings at 200 Hz, a human straightens bent nails, and its 50.5 N "impact force" comes from a push-pull gauge safety test, not from nail driving ([ARMADA](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16908)).

On the learning side, Grasp-to-Act is the most direct competitor. It uses a 16-DoF LEAP hand on a UR5 at **30 Hz**, with an RL residual that "issues joint corrections to prevent in-hand slip" across hammering, sawing, cutting, stirring and scooping. Its simulated nail is immovable, slip is measured by OptiTrack (0.69 cm and 1.35° on the real hammer), and no impact force or strike speed is reported ([Grasp-to-Act](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20466)). SimToolReal's 22-DoF Sharpa hand swings hammers as pose trajectories and warns that it "does not guarantee functional task completion, especially for high-force interactions". Drops make up 34.5% of its failures ([SimToolReal](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16863)). The physics-conditioned grasp planner iTuP selects grasps that minimise predicted interaction wrench during hammering and cuts induced torque by up to 17.6%, but it chooses *where* to grasp, not *how hard and when* ([Trupin, Wang, Qureshi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01399)). Tool-as-Interface drives a 3D-printed nail 13/13 times from RGB alone, with no force channel ([Tool-as-Interface](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04612v2)). Adroit Hammer remains simulation-only, with a nail whose friction absorbs up to 15 N ([Gymnasium-Robotics](https://robotics.farama.org/envs/adroit_hand/adroit_hammer/)).

Impact-aware control has matured without ever involving a held tool. Reference spreading (RS) moved from simulation to real dual-arm Franka experiments, which its authors call the "first time an experimental evaluation of reference spreading control on a robotic setup" ([van Steen et al., T-RO 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08643)). It then reached 600 hit-and-push and grabbing trials with time-invariant references ([arXiv 2411.09870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09870)), and post-impact velocity jumps were matched to 3.1% in simulation ([arXiv 2411.06319](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06319)). Impact-aware QP control bounds hardware-affordable impacts on Panda and HRP-4 ([Wang et al., IJRR 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01987)). In every case **the impacting body is the robot's own end effector or pad**. The July 2026 nonsmooth-impact-direction paper names "repetitive hammering" only as a future application ([Kirner & Ott](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13768)). Competitions confirm the gap is practical as well as academic. The 2026 World Humanoid Robot Games scored fully driven, unbent nails in five minutes, and a referee reported many robots driving nails at angles ([Bastille Post](https://www.bastillepost.com/global/article/6104693-robots-compete-to-hammer-nails-with-precision-at-humanoid-robot-games-in-beijing)).

## Novelty sits in the grasp-impact coupling, not the hierarchy or the sensors

The component-by-component ledger shows where reviewers will grant novelty and where they will refuse it. The broad claim "no system fuses a slow VLA with a fast tactile layer" is **false**. Reactive Diffusion Policy pairs a 1-2 Hz latent policy with a ~24 Hz tactile tokenizer ([RDP](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02881v3)). FAVLA schedules a fast force expert under a VLM and explicitly cites "delayed responses to impacts, stick-slip, and force spikes", yet trains on 30 Hz F/T for insertion and wiping ([FAVLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23648)). T-Rex re-triggers a tactile expert inside 30 Hz chunks over a 300 Hz control thread ([T-Rex](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17055v2)). Figure's Helix 02 runs a 200 Hz S1 on fingertip tactile input over a 1 kHz S0 ([Figure](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix-02)). The defensible residue is that every learned fast layer runs at **≤300 Hz** on tactile/force data downsampled to roughly 24-30 Hz, outputs kinematic targets, and has never faced an impulsive tool strike. The project's L2 differs on three axes: **input bandwidth** (raw kHz windows), **action space** (impedance plus grip force), and **regime** (millisecond impacts). Helix 02's S1 runs at the same 200 Hz, so the difference must be argued "to our knowledge, from public descriptions".

The action space is incremental. ACP outputs stiffness ([ACP](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.09309v2)). UMI-FT "predicts position targets, grasp force, and stiffness" ([UMI-FT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09988)). Imp-ACT predicts motion-direction stiffness, with 29x less contact-force vibration in wiping ([Imp-ACT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31225)). The sensing stack is close to SlipSense's TacV5, which pairs a 32×32 array at 240 Hz with an **8 kHz MEMS accelerometer** and reaches 96.7% macro-F1 at under 1.6% false positives ([SlipSense](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.15910)). SpectRobot already turns up to 200 kS/s vibration into spectrogram images for an ACT policy, though at 30 Hz ([SpectRobot](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24621v2)). VibeAct maps piezo waveforms to contact-onset and slip features for RL policies ([VibeAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27344)). Sensing novelty therefore comes only from the **impact regime**, not the modality.

Five contributions survive scrutiny. The first is the **anticipatory grip ramp**. No robotics paper schedules a grip-force increase ahead of a predicted impulsive load sized from tool mass, strike speed and friction. iTuP selects grasp placement, Grasp-to-Act learns black-box joint residuals, and IMPACT feeds forward *arm* wrench, not grip ([IMPACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.10818)). The second is **impact control through a held tool**. RS, impact-aware QP and momentum-observer detection have never been applied where the impulse passes through a compliant, slip-prone grasp. There the rigid impact map is ill-defined, which is exactly the decoupling Ti et al. reported. Coupling impact handling with grip-force modulation has no precedent at all. The third is the **slip-detector evaluation under impacts**. SlipSense's negative classes include gripper actuation and pick-and-place but no tool strikes, even though its authors note that actuation "generate[s] strong vibration responses". The reflex bars of ~23 ms (SlipSense) and ~20-30 ms ([Ayral, Aloui, Grossard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16127)) are still about 5x longer than a ~4 ms blow. That makes "adjust the next strike" a principled design rather than a concession, and the project's finding that patch-only drop detection false-triggers in a wrap grasp is a publishable negative result. The fourth is the **actuator-feasibility audit**. Grasp-to-Act and SimToolReal never report actuator or gearbox load against rating. The literature on self-locking finger drives claims they resist force "to the limit of the strength of the materials" ([US 4957320](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4957320)). The project's 20-40x-rating result quantifies what that phrase means under a hammer impulse. It is sharper because WUJI moved from non-backdrivable drives in generation 1 to back-drivable direct drive in Hand 2 ([MIDAS Hand comparison](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14487); [WUJI Hand 2](https://www.wuji.tech/en/hand2)). The fifth is **force-truth data**. No public dataset pairs per-strike impact force, depth increment and in-grasp slip with kHz streams. REASSEMBLE and RH20T are quasi-static, and the TU/e I.AM. impact database has no tools and no slip ([REASSEMBLE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05086); [I.AM. database](https://impact-aware-robotics-database.tue.nl/datasets)).

Two parts are weaker than they look. Across-strike margin adaptation is a plausible but less clear-cut novelty, because Grasp-to-Act's online RL already reduces cumulative slip across repeated strikes. The distinction has to be an **explicit, interpretable per-strike update**, such as ILC or a Bayesian friction posterior driven by tactile and vibration evidence, plus regrasp decisions between strikes; no paper found does either. The limit-aware braking swing with arc strikes is new for rigid torque-controlled arms. Prior maximum-velocity work relies on variable-stiffness actuators: the VSA hammer gained about 30% head speed ([Garabini et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221065759_Optimality_principles_in_variable_stiffness_control_The_VSA_hammer)). But it is a planning detail, not a headline.

## Reviewers will attack the VLA, the simulator and the borrowed controllers

The first attack will be that **the hierarchy is not new and the VLA is ornamental**. A reviewer who knows RDP, FAVLA, T-Rex, AT-VLA, PhaForce and Helix 02 will ask what a 1-10 Hz language model adds to driving one nail, and the honest answer is "phase and target selection", which a script also provides. The defence is to state the VLA's role as an interface and to show that the contribution survives with a scripted planner. Otherwise the paper must also run VLA baselines (π0-class, FAVLA), which it cannot win on a single task.

The second attack is **"you applied known controllers"**. Reference spreading with an interim mode is van Steen et al.'s ([arXiv 2305.08643](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08643)). Gating velocity feedback during ringing is conceptually their interim mode, which runs "without using velocity error feedback" ([arXiv 2212.00877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.00877)), and Yang and Posa's impact-invariant projection ([arXiv 2303.00817](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00817)). The momentum observer is the standard collision residual ([Haddadin et al., T-RO 2017](http://www.diag.uniroma1.it/~labrob/pub/papers/TRO_Collision_Dec2017.pdf)). The paper should cite these as the method's sources and claim only what the held-tool setting forces to change. That includes an impact map through a compliant grasp, repeated rather than single impacts, and detection that must separate intended strikes from slip and from ringing.

The third attack targets **simulation fidelity**. MuJoCo's soft constraints "limit accuracy for rigid contacts and stick-slip transitions", and stiffening them past a threshold destabilises the solver ([MuJoCo docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/modeling.html)). Simulators capture inelastic impacts reasonably but "generally fail to reproduce elasticity" ([arXiv 2110.00541](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.00541)). A reviewer will therefore suspect that the 20-40x gearbox overload is an artefact of rigid gears and soft contact. The defence is twofold. First, report results as **relative comparisons** (rated vs self-locking vs parallel gripper) that hold under contact-parameter sweeps. Second, validate peak force, impulse and rebound against the real rig, following van Steen's 3.1% post-impact-velocity check.

The fourth attack is **attribution**. White et al.'s ~65 ms grip peak comes from **targeted tapping with a hand-held object**, not hammering ([White et al. 2011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306452211004957)). The peak delay depends on contact stiffness: about 40 ms after peak load for stiff fields and about 4 ms for soft ones ([White et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurorobotics/articles/10.3389/fnbot.2018.00031/full)). "Human hammering grip" must become "human impact and tapping paradigms". The WUJI Hand 2 per-joint torque ratings (~0.2-2 Nm) have no public source, because the product page lists only a ≥12 N fingertip force ([WUJI Hand 2](https://www.wuji.tech/en/hand2)). Every rating in the feasibility claim therefore needs a datasheet or SDK citation, or it becomes the easiest target in the paper.

The fifth attack concerns **actuation latency and statistics**. The Franka Hand's `grasp()` is a high-level call outside the 1 kHz arm loop. One user requested 25 N and measured a 35 N peak settling to ~5 N, and the issue was closed without a technical answer ([libfranka issue #136](https://github.com/frankarobotics/libfranka/issues/136)). If L2 commands a grip ramp the gripper cannot track, the anticipatory claim collapses, so the paper must measure the gripper's step response itself and schedule the ramp at least that far ahead. On statistics, Grasp-to-Act sets the floor at 10 real trials per task and 3 simulation seeds ([Grasp-to-Act](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20466v1)). A credible paper reports 20 or more trials per condition with per-strike distributions and confidence intervals, across at least two hammer masses and handle diameters.

## One predictor can span impulses, jerks and oscillations if split by predictability and bandwidth

The user's follow-up asks whether impacts, jerks and oscillations can be merged into one framework. They can, and the cleanest version is a **phase-indexed internal model that predicts the grip wrench over the task cycle**. A single predictor drives both the grip-force setpoint and the arm impedance. Each load component is routed by two properties, how predictable its timing is and how fast it is, rather than by task name. The human evidence supports one predictive mechanism across load types. Grip force "was modulated in parallel with, and thus anticipated, fluctuations in load force" for inertial, viscous, elastic and composite loads ([Flanagan & Wing 1997](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/17/4/1519)), building on their point-to-point movement work ([Flanagan & Wing 1993](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00229662)). The same anticipatory scaling appears in collisions: grip rises before contact and is "scaled to the predicted LF magnitude", more strongly for self-produced impacts ([Turrell, Li & Wing 1999](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s002210050822)). Grip-force rate stays positive for about 200 ms before impact ([White et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurorobotics/articles/10.3389/fnbot.2018.00031/full)). Load force in collisions rises "in less than 20 ms", so "only predictive control mechanisms can compensate" ([Delevoye-Turrell, Li & Wing 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/02724980245000025)). Throwers scale grip to ball weight and intended speed through the backswing ([Hore et al. 2001](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/jn.2001.86.6.2678)), and in simulated collisions the whole grip profile is time-locked, with only magnitude and rate adjusted ([Kuling, Salmen & Lefèvre 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00221-019-05606-y)).

Two human findings caution against a naive single law, and they also justify the frequency split. Grip-load coupling becomes **intermittent during oscillation** of a hand-held object ([Exp Brain Res 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00221-018-5315-2)). Humans also **switch strategy** with load stiffness, from synchronous peaks for soft loads to a ~40-60 ms lag for stiff ones ([White et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurorobotics/articles/10.3389/fnbot.2018.00031/full)). The nervous system therefore runs one predictor but changes how it is expressed by regime, which is what the proposed routing does.

| Load content | Example | Predictable timing? | Bandwidth | Handling in the unified controller |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slow periodic | sawing stroke, swing inertial load | yes | < 10 Hz | internal-model feedforward + feedback on grip and arm wrench |
| Timed impulse | hammer strike, ~4 ms pulse | yes (contact time known ±) | > 100 Hz | pre-set grip/stiffness before contact; post-impact feedback gating; RS references |
| Untimed jerk | screw cam-out, drill breakthrough | no | 10-100 Hz edge | standing margin + fast reflex (~20-30 ms) + pad/wrist compliance |
| Fast oscillation | drill chatter, hammer-drill 10-100 Hz blows | statistically, not per cycle | 50-500 Hz | passive pad/wrist compliance; grip raised to stiffen hand impedance |
| Cycle-to-cycle drift | friction change, tool creep | slow | per stroke | margin adaptation (ILC/Bayesian) from tactile/vibration evidence |
| All | — | — | — | passivity / energy-tank layer bounding injected energy from stiffness and grip changes |

The table shows where the framework adds value and where it does not. For timed impulses and slow periodic loads, prediction is the whole story, and the framework improves on reactive control by construction. For untimed jerks such as cam-out and breakthrough, prediction contributes only a standing margin. Even expert spine surgeons need 0.226 ± 0.181 s to react to drill breakthrough through the tool's feel, against 0.020 s for automated haptic detection ([spine-drilling study, PMC9837054](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9837054/)). The honest claim is that the framework *allocates* those events to compliance and reflex, not that it predicts them. Fast oscillation such as chatter is beyond any loop at these rates. The ergonomics evidence that higher grip raises hand-arm impedance and lowers transmitted vibration ([Lindenmann et al. 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003687021000776)) makes grip force a *stiffness knob* in that band, echoing White's conclusion that grip regulates hand stiffness and damping. The passivity layer is needed because variable stiffness itself injects energy, which is why Imp-ACT calls for a passivity filter ([Imp-ACT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31225)).

The unification is only moderately novel. **IMPACT** already learns an internal model that predicts interaction force from state and action history and applies it as feedforward, across tools of varying weight and wiping. It covers the arm wrench under quasi-static loads, not grip and not impulses ([IMPACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.10818)). **Grasp-to-Act** already uses one controller for "impacts, torques, and continuous resistance" across hammering and sawing. It is a black-box 30 Hz RL residual with no explicit phase or frequency structure ([Grasp-to-Act](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20466)). **Tracing Energy Flow** learns tactile grasp-force control against slippage in dynamic object interaction, but it is reactive and model-predictive rather than anticipatory of impulses. Its PDF could not be parsed, so its details are unverified ([arXiv 2512.21043](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.21043)). What none of them has, and what the unified controller would add, is **one explicit, phase-indexed grip-wrench predictor that drives grip and arm impedance together, with predictability-by-bandwidth routing and a passivity bound**. That is a real conceptual contribution, but reviewers will call it a framework paper unless it is shown on at least two load classes with the same code and only parameters changed. Hammering plus sawing, or hammering plus screw driving with cam-out, would suffice. The right home is a T-RO or IJRR long paper in 2027-28, after the hammering paper establishes the anticipatory-grip result.

## Split into three papers and aim the first at RSS 2027

The strongest framing for the first paper is **"Predict the blow: anticipatory grip-and-impedance control for impacts through a held tool"**. It leads with the physics argument: a ~4 ms blow, gripper actuation of tens to hundreds of milliseconds, and slip reflexes of 20-30 ms mean feedback cannot save the current strike. Its three claims are the anticipatory ramp, impact handling through the grasp, and per-strike margin adaptation, all validated on the FR3 force-truth rig with the MuJoCo testbed as the design and analysis tool. The VLA becomes a phase/target interface, scripted in the main experiments. **RSS 2027** fits unusually well. Its new two-stage process judges a 6-page extended abstract due **December 4, 2026** on hypothesis and methodology. It explicitly states that "submissions will not be rejected for yielding negative experimental results". A Stage-2 "Extension Charter" runs until the April 16, 2027 final paper ([RSS 2027 CfP](https://roboticsconference.org/information/cfp/)). The simulator findings can carry Stage 1, and the real rig carries Stage 2. A fitting pre-registered hypothesis is that anticipatory grip reduces per-strike slip without reducing depth per strike. The fallback is **CoRL 2027**, whose deadline is expected around late May 2027 by pattern (CoRL 2026 was May 28; [CoRL](https://www.corl.org/contributions/instruction-for-authors)). ICRA 2027's September 16, 2026 deadline has passed, and Humanoids 2026 closed in July ([ICRA 2027](https://2027.ieee-icra.org/contribute/call-for-icra-2027-papers-now-accepting-submissions/); [Humanoids 2026](https://2026.ieee-humanoids.org/call-for-papers/)).

The second paper, **"Can a dexterous hand hold a hammer?"**, packages the hardware-limit-faithful findings. A power wrap at rated torques fails, self-locking drives hold only by loading gearboxes 20-40x their rating, patch-only drop detection false-triggers, and a 100 Hz position-only humanoid interface caps strike speed near 1.1 m/s. This is a feasibility and negative-results paper suited to **RA-L** (rolling) or **Humanoids 2027** (about July by pattern). It needs sourced actuator ratings, contact-parameter sensitivity sweeps, and at least one real anchor, such as matching simulated impulse on the FR3 rig. Kept separate, it stops the first paper from carrying three hands and two platforms. The third paper is the **force-truth dataset**. An IJRR data paper must demonstrate use with an existing public algorithm and "should not present a new algorithm" ([IJRR guidelines](https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/ijr)), so it follows the controller paper and uses a published baseline. The unified load-predictive framework is the fourth paper, for T-RO.

The first paper needs the experiments below. Baselines should include a fixed maximum grip, a fixed nominal grip, reactive-only slip control at the SlipSense/Ayral reflex rate, a Grasp-to-Act-style learned residual where feasible, a stiff impedance controller without RS or gating, and RS without grip coupling. ARMADA's human figure (13.15 strikes per 20 mm) serves as a reference point.

## Conclusion

The project's value lies less in any single layer than in a shift of question. The field has asked how to *execute* a hammer trajectory or *react* to slip. This project asks how a grasp should be *prepared* for an impulse it cannot react to in time, and what hardware can survive that impulse at all. The first question has a human-grounded answer that no robot implements, and the second has an uncomfortable quantitative answer for dexterous hands that their builders have not published. Both are stronger paper claims than "a VLA with a fast tactile layer", which the field has already populated. The decisive asset is the force-truth rig. Without it, every novel claim rests on a simulator that reviewers distrust at exactly the timescale that matters. With it, the anticipatory-grip result and the slip-under-impact false-positive result become hard to dispute.

The unifying load-predictive controller is best understood as the project's research programme rather than its first paper. Its sharpest version is honest about its limits: prediction governs timed and periodic loads, compliance and reflexes absorb untimed jerks and chatter, and grip force doubles as an impedance knob across both. Once hammering is established, adding one oscillatory or jerk-dominated task on the same rig turns that taxonomy into a testable claim. That separates the project from IMPACT's arm-only feedforward and Grasp-to-Act's black-box residual. The work for the next nine weeks is the December 4 RSS abstract, with pre-registered hypotheses and the rig as the Extension Charter.
